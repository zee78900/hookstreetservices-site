Start with intake and a calendar, not a robot

Your bottleneck isn’t reply speed. It’s that nothing gets logged and nothing has a date on it. Fix those two and AI drafting has something real to work with. Three moves below, run in order.

What we heard

The setup: 12 units across three small buildings a few blocks apart. One owner, we’ll call him Danny (invented name, like everything else on this page), plus a part-time handyman he pays by Zelle. No office, no software, no staff.

Maintenance: every request lands as a text or call on Danny’s personal phone, at any hour. Nothing is sorted by urgency: the loose cabinet hinge and the no-heat call arrive the same way and compete for the same attention. Nothing is logged, so “I told you about this last month” can’t be checked, and twice last year a small leak sat long enough to become a drywall repair.

Leases and deposits: the 12 leases end in eight different months. Renewal letters go out when Danny remembers; last year two leases quietly rolled month-to-month because the notice window slipped by. Move-outs mean reconstructing the deposit math from photos and memory, against a state deadline for the itemized letter (commonly 14 to 30 days, check yours).

January: 1099 season means scrolling twelve months of Zelle and checks to figure out what the handyman, the plumber, and the landscaper were actually paid. It ate most of a weekend last year. (One for your accountant: the 1099 reporting threshold moves from $600 to $2,000 starting with 2026 payments, so confirm who you actually owe forms to.)

The real question under the question: Danny asked if AI could answer his tenants for him. It can draft, and that’s worth real hours. But full auto-reply is the wrong first move for a 12-unit operation built on personal relationships, and we talked through why below.

What not to automate

  • Anything that reaches a tenant without your eyes. Drafts yes, auto-send no. Your relationships are the business; one wrong automated message costs more than all the minutes saved.
  • Emergencies. No heat in winter, gas smell, water where it shouldn’t be: that’s a phone call and, in most states, a legal duty with a clock on it. Keep a human line that skips every workflow, and tell tenants exactly what it is.
  • Tenant screening and approvals. Regulators are watching AI tenant screening closely; there is federal fair-housing guidance on it. AI can assemble the file; the decision, and the paper trail showing you applied the same standard to everyone, stays with you.
  • Deposit deductions and hardship calls. AI can pull the photos, the ledger, and the move-in checklist into one place. What to charge, and whether to work with a tenant in a rough patch, is your judgment; in many states, wrongly keeping a deposit can cost you two or three times the amount.
  • A full property-management platform, for now. The big platforms carry minimums near $300 a month, and even the mid-tier ones start around $60. At 12 units, switching over costs more than it gives back. Revisit around 25 to 30 units, after intake runs clean.
